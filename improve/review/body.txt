One Step Ahead Is Not Enough: Testing a Learned
Quadruped Simulator at the Horizon It Is Used
Anonymous Author(s)
Abstract—Learned simulators let legged robots learn in long
imagined rollouts, yet are often trained and checked one step
ahead. We rebuilt the open quadruped simulator of the Robotic
World Model (RWM) and measured 101 trained simulators and
200 compressed copies. First, one-step checks picked the wrong
choices (descriptive): teacher forcing and one-step training were
better one step ahead but 2.0 and 2.6 times as wrong at 2 s, the
100-step horizon RWM imagines, and pruning 30% of weights
kept 90% of the skill one step ahead but 39% at 2 s. Second, fine-
tuning restored the 30%-pruned copies’ skill (registered). Third,
checks needing no recorded future only partly replaced checks
several steps ahead (registered tests): on 97 GRU simulators the
error growth rate ranked them by 2-second error better than
one-step error, but was not separated from 8-step error and failed
a replication (secondary); a kinematic residual ranked them better
than one-step error, but less well under policy-chosen commands.
We recommend checking 8 or more steps ahead on held-out
episodes, with these checks as a fallback. Scope: simulated data,
one robot and trotting gait, no physics-simulator test.
Index Terms—learned simulators, world models, legged robots,
model-based reinforcement learning, Lyapunov exponents, model
compression, uncertainty
I. INTRODUCTION
A legged robot can learn to walk inside alearned simulator:
a neural network that predicts the robot’s next state from its
recent states andjoint commands(the 12 joint position targets
given at every step), run forward on its own predictions, as
image-based world models are [ 1]. Such a run is arollout, and
its length is itshorizon; because each prediction becomes the
next input, small errors can grow [ 2–5]. The Robotic World
Model (RWM) [ 6] learns such a simulator for an ANYmal D
quadruped and trains a walking policy inside it in imagined
rollouts of 100 steps (2 s) per update [ 6, Table S11], as does
RWM-U [7] (100 steps). RWM trains on its own 8-step rollouts;
its ablation found this better than teacher forcing, and the
forecast horizon decisive for long-term accuracy [ 6, Sec. 4.3,
App. A.4.1].
Elsewhere, one-step checks remain common. Offline model-
based methods build on ensembles of one-step models [ 8, 9],
and RAMBO keeps the members with the lowest error on held-
out single transitions [ 10]. Compression [ 11] is a standard way
to shrink networks, and ensemble uncertainty [8, 12] is often
judged on held-out one-step error, although one-step accuracy
need not track what a model is used for [ 13]. We ask two
questions.When does a one-step check give the wrong answer
about a simulator used 100 steps ahead?Andcan a cheap
check that needs no recorded future states do better?
We answer on RWM’s open-source dynamics model, in a
narrow setting: one robot, one trotting gait on flat ground,
simulated data, and no test of a trained policy in a physics
simulator. The main comparisons of checks were registered
before their measurements were run. Our first results agree with
RWM’s ablation and add the one-step view, other architectures
and a policy in the loop. Our contributions are:
• Where one-step checks mislead(Sec. IV-A): one step
ahead, teacher forcing, one-step training and pruning
without retraining look better or nearly harmless; at the
horizon of use they are worse, also after longer training
and in two of three other architectures (descriptive). Fine-
tuning repairs the 30%-pruned copies.
• A registered comparison of checks that need no
recorded future(Sec. IV-B): the growth rate of small
errors and a kinematic residual against one-step and 8-
step error, on two pools, three other architectures and two
sources of joint commands.
• A checklistfor vetting a learned simulator, led by checks
several steps ahead (Sec. V).
II. RELATEDWORK
Compounding error.Training on a model’s own pre-
dictions [ 2, 3, 14, 15] and limiting rollout length [ 5] are
established remedies for errors that grow along a rollout; one-
step likelihood need not track control performance (objective
mismatch[ 13]).Uncertainty for model-based control.En-
sembles [12, 16] estimate uncertainty; offline methods penalise
imagined reward by their disagreement [ 7, 8], and compare
penalties by how well they track error [ 17]; recalibration
corrects predicted uncertainty on held-out data [ 18, 19].
Stability of recurrent networks.Products of Jacobians govern
exploding gradients [ 20]; contracting recurrent models are
easier to train [ 21]; Lyapunov spectra characterise chaotic
recurrent networks [ 22] and their training difficulty, which
modified teacher forcing addresses [ 23, 24], or stability is
built in [ 25].Physics and compression.Like our kinematic
residual, iKCE [ 26] asks, without a recorded future, whether
imagined positions follow from imagined velocities (cf. parity
relations [27]); it diagnosed one model (DreamerV3 on DMC
walker-walk), whereas we use the residual to rank many
simulators. Compressed world models are judged by task
success [28] or error growth [29], and pruning is often matched
by small networks trained from scratch [30].
III. SYSTEM ANDPROTOCOL
Data.The release contains 10,000 recorded steps of an
ANYmal D trotting on flat ground at 50 Hz: ten 20-second
episodes, in each of which thevelocity command(the walking
speed and turning rate the robot is asked to follow) appears to
change once. Every episode starts from an exact reset, so the
data come from a physics simulation, not the hardware. Each
step holds a 45-number state (body velocities, the direction of
gravity seen from the body, and each joint’s angle, speed and
torque) and the 12 joint commands. The main pool’s training
episodes hold 7,991 transitions, 0.13% of the 6 million RWM
pretrained on [6, Table 1].
Simulator and rebuild.The network is RWM’s: two layers
of gated recurrent units (GRU, a standard recurrent layer) of
width 256 read the last 32 states and joint commands and
predict the change to the next state. RWM trains it on its own
8-step rollouts (rollout training); its released checkpoint has
five output heads on one shared trunk, whose spread is the
uncertainty RWM-U uses. Our rebuild reproduces the release’s
outputs bit for bit and its gradients exactly, and avoids two
defects of the release: training windows spliced across episodes,
and evaluation commands fed one step late (appendix A1).
Held-out data and pools.Each held-out episode yields two
non-overlapping trajectories of 32 history and 368 forecast steps
(7.36 s). Themain pool(F18) holds out episodes 1 and 8 (four
trajectories) and has 97 simulators. Thefive-split pool(X5)
holds each episode out once (20 trajectories, 255 simulators,
each scored on its own split).
Models.The main pool has 48 rollout-trained models: five
seeds of the released configuration (history M= 32 , training
horizon N= 8 ) and variants such as ensembles, a likelihood
loss and other (M, N)settings. 9 were trained one step ahead,
byteacher forcing[ 31] (each step predicted from the true
previous state, never from the model’s own prediction) or
with N= 1 . The other 40 are compressed copies, made after
training: weights rounded to 16 or 8 bits, the smallest 10–
50% of weights set to zero (pruning), or GRU units removed.
The five-split pool repeats the main training methods and
compressions on every split. The main pool also holds teacher-
forced and rollout-trained models trained four times longer.
Counted once, the 101 trained and 200 compressed GRU
simulators fill both pools: the main pool’s 97 are 57 trained
models and 40 copies, the five-split pool’s 255 are 55 and 200,
11 trained models and the 40 copies are in both (F18’s split
is one of the five), and study AR adds 38 simulators of three
other architectures trained on F18’s split. Training used the
release’s loss and Adam for 2,500 iterations unless stated, on
a CPU (appendix A1).
Measures.Error is the mean absolute error over the 45
state numbers, each divided by its standard deviation in the
data. Eh is the mean error over h-step rollouts started, after
32 true history steps, from every fourth held-out position ( E1:
every position);E 368 uses one 368-step rollout per trajectory.
Thefloorpredicts no change, holding the last history state. A
compressed copy’sskill retainedis K(h) = 1−∆(h)/S(h) ,
where ∆ is its extra error over its parent and S the parent’s
advantage over the floor; K <0 is worse than predicting no
change. Two checks arelabel-free: they need the history and the
joint commands but no recorded future state. Thegrowth rate
λ1, the leading finite-time Lyapunov exponent, is the average
exponential rate per second at which the gap between two
copies of the simulator, started from almost identical internal
states and given the same joint commands, grows ( λ1 >0 )
or shrinks ( <0 , acontractingsimulator); we compute it over
each 368-step rollout by Benettin’s method [ 32]. Thekinematic
residual ¯rJ asks whether predicted joint angles change as the
predicted joint speeds say, each joint in units of its residual in
the training episodes, averaged over the rollout.
Statistics and registration.Intervals are 95% percentile
intervals from 20,000 bootstrap draws over the independent
unit: whole trajectories for comparisons of methods [ 33, 34],
models for correlations. A result isregisteredif its decision
rule was committed to version control before the measurements
that test it were run [ 35]; a rule gives a verdict only when
its interval clears a stated threshold (usually zero), and “not
settled” otherwise. Three registrations were made for this paper:
the comparison of checks on both pools (primary Q1–Q2,
secondary Q3–Q6), study FT on fine-tuning pruned copies
(FT1–FT2), and studies CL and AR on policy commands and
other architectures (CL1–CL2, AR1–AR2). Primary questions
are judged at 97.5%. The registrations are commits in our
own version control, so their times are self-reported: the
supplementary history shows their order but no independent
timestamp, and the answer to Q1 was largely predictable from
earlier analyses (appendix A2). Other verdicts come from rules
registered for earlier, unpublished analyses; those registrations
and all code are in the anonymised supplementary material.
Secondary verdicts are judged at 95% without correction for
running several tests; Holm’s correction over the 7 secondary
tests of Q3–Q6 is exploratory. Results are markedregistered
(primary or secondary; main verdicts in Table III),descriptive
(measured, not tested) orexploratory(added after the registered
results were seen). Resampling parents and trajectories together
widens the intervals by a median factor of 1.5 (exploratory,
appendix A4).

Stability.The growth rate tracks the training horizon: it is
positive for N= 1 (+0.61/s) and falls through +0.44, −0.43
and −1.13 to −1.38/s at N= 32 (descriptive; a rule committed
before that analysis found the same order, appendix A1). Every
rebuilt model trained on its own rollouts of eight or more steps
was contracting, and no teacher-forced one was. The released
model does not fit: trained on unrecorded data (RWM reports
about 750 times our transitions), it has λ1 = +0.70/s on these
trajectories, which it may have trained on (appendix A6).
Compression.Pruning 30% of weights without retraining
keeps 90% of the parent’s skill one step ahead but 39% at 100
steps, and by 368 steps the copy is worse than predicting no
change (descriptive). In a registered follow-up (appendix A7),
500 iterations of fine-tuning with the pruned weights held at
zero restored the 100-step skill of seeds 0–2 from 38% to
102.2%, and the fine-tuned copies were nearly as skilled as
their parents fine-tuned the same way (99.9% [99.5%, 100.4%];
Table III). Skill above 100% mostly reflects the extra training,
not the pruning (descriptive). Moderate pruning adds error, but
at 30% the registered test could not separate the shift in growth
rate (+0.03/s [−0.02, +0.07]) from zero (not settled; secondary),
and no copy pruned by 10–30% has λ1 >0 (appendix A5).
Rounding weights to 8 bits keeps 97.0% of the 100-step skill
(descriptive; weights only).
Uncertainty(descriptive). The released ensemble’s spread
ranks error well (Spearman +0.90 with the 100-step error, on
episodes it may have trained on) but is too narrow, by a factor
of 6.6 one step ahead and 30.8 at 100 steps, so a one-step
calibration check understates the problem; each head’s own
variance collapses (appendix A6).

errors E1 and E8; study CL asked whether policy commands

Five-split pool.The registered replication on the five-
split pool, where most of the 51 simulators per split are
compressed copies, failed for the growth rate: one-step error
ranked better than it, and the 8-step error better still (secondary,
Table III). The first reading fails Holm’s correction and the
wider intervals, but the growth rate beat one-step error in no
reading (exploratory, appendix A4). A possible cause, not tested
here, is that moderate pruning adds error without a shift in
growth rate that we could detect (Q6); a negative growth rate
certifies nothing: of the 17 main-pool simulators worse than
the floor at 100 steps, 15 had λ1 ≤0 . The kinematic residual,
registered on the main pool only, was level with the 8-step
error here (+0.01 [−0.01, +0.03]; exploratory).
Other architectures.Study AR repeated the two compar-
isons that had favoured the label-free checks on 38 runs of
an MLP, an RSSM and a Transformer simulator, ranking
within each architecture and averaging. The growth rate ranked
better than one-step error (AR1), carried by the MLP and the
Transformer, where one-step error ranked the runs largely in
reverse; in the RSSM, one-step error ranked them better (+0.88
against +0.26; descriptive). The residual against the 8-step error
(AR2) was not settled (Table III). Ranked across architectures
together, the growth rate did not rank the runs ( ρ=− 0.11;
secondary, appendix A9).
Policy commands.Study CL let the RWM authors’ released
policy, not the recording, supply the joint commands: at each
step it read the simulator’s own predicted state and a fixed
velocity command. Such a rollout has no recorded future, so the
checks were ranked against E100 under recorded commands.
The residual then ranked less well (+0.83 against +0.95), a drop
carried by the trained models and seen again on the five-split
pool (secondary); the growth rate’s change was not settled
(Table III). With small noise added to the recorded commands
instead, both checks were unchanged, and differentiated through
the policy as well (closed loop), the growth rate did not rank the
simulators (secondary, appendix A8). Simulators with higher
error gave the policy lower imagined return ( ρ=− 0.86;
secondary). This signal needs a policy and a reward but
no recording, and was nominally the strongest under policy
commands, ahead of the residual’s +0.83 (descriptive); it was
not registered as a check and is a candidate for a follow-up
study.
V. DISCUSSION
Why one-step checks mislead here.A one-step check
feeds a simulator true recorded states; in use it reads its own
predictions, so what matters is how it treats its own errors.
Teacher forcing and one-step training never show the network
those errors, and their simulators amplify them ( λ1 >0 );
pruning without retraining may add a small error at each step
that accumulates.
A checklist from this case study.(1)Check several steps
ahead on held-out episodes: judge a simulator by its error 8
or more steps ahead, ideally at the horizon of use, counting
independent trajectories. One-step error picked the wrong
training choices, and no label-free check was shown better
than 8-step error in a primary test (not settled; Table III).
(2)Fine-tune after pruning, and check the pruned copy at the
horizon of use. (3)Without recordings, fall back on label-free
checks and know their limits: the residual ranked the main

Limitations.All evidence comes from one robot, one trotting
gait on flat ground and ten 20-second episodes of simulated data,
so “held out” means other velocity commands of the same gait.
The policy drove the simulators only in imagination, against
error under recorded commands: no policy was trained or tested
in a physics simulator or on hardware, so what a mis-ranked
simulator costs a controller is not measured, and imagined
return is not walking performance. The main pool’s four held-
out trajectories from two episodes bound every interval, and no
correction is made across the three registrations. Correlations
treat models as independent although they share parents, data
and test trajectories; wider intervals that allow for this leave
3 of 13 registered verdicts of the two GRU pools unresolved
(Q1 at 24 steps, Q3 against E1, Q5 against E1; exploratory).
The growth rate is a finite-time exponent in the network’s
own coordinates, and the stability account does not cover the
released model. Each other architecture has 12 or 13 runs (3
teacher-forced MLP runs diverged on some trajectories under
recorded commands, kept as registered), and the longer-training
check has 3 seeds and no intervals.
VI. CONCLUSION
On this simulator, one-step checks favoured teacher forcing,
one-step training and a pruned model that, without fine-tuning,
loses most of its skill by 2 s. Checking 8 or more steps ahead
on held-out episodes is the safer test. Without recorded futures,
the growth rate beat one-step error on the main pool but failed
its replication (secondary), and the kinematic residual ranked
best on the main pool with recorded commands (descriptive)
but less well under a policy. Whether these checks predict how
well a policy trained inside the simulator walks is the next
question.
