One Step Ahead Is Not Enough: Testing a Learned
Quadruped Simulator at the Horizon It Is Used
Anonymous Author(s)
Abstract—Learned simulators let legged robots learn in long
imagined rollouts, yet are often trained and checked one step
ahead. We rebuilt the open quadruped simulator of the Robotic
World Model (RWM) and measured 101 trained simulators and
200 compressed copies. One-step checks picked the wrong training
choices: teacher forcing and one-step training were better one step
ahead but 2.0 and 2.6 times as wrong at 2 s (descriptive; partly
registered earlier), also after longer training and in two of three
other architectures. Pruning 30% of weights kept 90% of the
skill one step ahead, 39% at 2 s (descriptive); fine-tuning restored
nearly all of it (registered). The error growth rate, needing no
recorded future, ranked simulators by 2-second error better than
one-step error (registered) on 97 GRU simulators and, averaged,
over three other architectures (two carry it). On the GRU pool
it was not separated from 8-step error (registered) and failed a
secondary replication on mostly compressed copies. A kinematic
residual ranked them best with recorded commands (descriptive;
its lead over 8-step error failed multiple-test correction), and less
well under policy-chosen commands (registered). Scope: simulated
data, one robot and trotting gait, no physics-simulator test.
Index Terms—learned simulators, world models, legged robots,
model-based reinforcement learning, Lyapunov exponents, model
compression, uncertainty
I. INTRODUCTION
A legged robot can learn to walk inside alearned simulator:
a neural network that predicts the robot’s next state from its
recent states andjoint commands(the 12 joint position targets
given at every step), run forward on its own predictions, as
image-based world models are [ 1]. Such a run is arollout, and
its length is itshorizon; because each prediction becomes the
next input, small errors can grow [ 2–5]. The Robotic World
Model (RWM) [ 6] learns such a simulator for an ANYmal D
quadruped and imagines 24 steps per policy update; RWM-
U [7] trains a walking policy inside 100-step imagined rollouts.
RWM trains on its own 8-step rollouts; its ablation found this
better than teacher forcing, and the forecast horizon decisive
for long-term accuracy [6, Sec. 4.3, App. A.4.1].
Elsewhere, one-step checks remain common. Offline model-
based methods build on ensembles of one-step models [ 8, 9],
and RAMBO keeps the members with the lowest error on held-
out single transitions [ 10]. Compression [ 11] is a standard way
to shrink networks, and ensemble uncertainty [8, 12] is often
judged on held-out one-step error, although one-step accuracy
need not track what a model is used for [ 13]. We ask two
questions.When does a one-step check give the wrong answer
about a simulator used 100 steps ahead?Andcan a cheap
check that needs no recorded future states do better?
We answer on RWM’s open-source dynamics model, in a
narrow setting: one robot, one trotting gait on flat ground,
simulated data, and no test of a trained policy in a physics
simulator. We rebuilt the model, matched the release’s gra-
dients exactly, and measured 101 trained simulators and 200
compressed copies. The main comparisons of checks were
registered before their measurements were run; additions are
marked exploratory. Our first two results agree with RWM’s
ablation; we add the one-step view, other architectures and a
policy in the loop. Our contributions are:
• Where one-step checks mislead(Sec. IV-A): judged
one step ahead, teacher forcing, one-step training and
pruning without retraining look better or nearly harmless;
at the horizon of use they are worse. The reversal persists
after four times longer training and in two of three other
architectures, and fine-tuning repairs the pruned copies
(registered).
• A registered comparison of checks that need no
recorded future(Sec. IV-B): the growth rate of small
errors and a kinematic residual against one-step and 8-step
error, on two pools of RWM-style simulators, three other
architectures and two sources of joint commands.
• A checklistfor vetting a learned simulator before long
imagined rollouts (Sec. V).
II. RELATEDWORK
Compounding error.Training on a model’s own pre-
dictions [ 2, 3, 14, 15] and limiting rollout length [ 5] are
established remedies for errors that grow along a rollout; one-
step likelihood need not track control performance (objective
mismatch[ 13]).Uncertainty for model-based control.En-
sembles [12, 16] estimate uncertainty; offline methods keep the
members with the lowest held-out one-step error [ 10], penalise
imagined reward by their disagreement [ 7, 8], and compare
penalties by how well they track error [ 17]; recalibration
corrects predicted uncertainty on held-out data [ 18, 19].
Stability of recurrent networks.Products of Jacobians govern
exploding gradients [ 20]; contracting recurrent models are
easier to train [ 21]; Lyapunov spectra characterise chaotic
recurrent networks [ 22] and their training difficulty, which
modified teacher forcing addresses [ 23, 24]; stable dynamics
can be learned by construction [ 25].Physics and compression.
Kinematic consistency has diagnosed long-horizon failure of
imagined rollouts [ 26], like parity relations [ 27]; compressed
world models are judged by task success [ 28] or error
growth [29], and pruning is often matched by small networks
trained from scratch [30].
III. SYSTEM ANDPROTOCOL
Data.The release contains 10,000 recorded steps of an
ANYmal D trotting on flat ground at 50 Hz: ten 20-second
episodes, in each of which thevelocity command(the walking
speed and turning rate the robot is asked to follow) appears to
change once. Every episode starts from an exact reset, so the
data come from a physics simulation, not the hardware. Each
step holds a 45-number state (body velocities, the direction of
gravity seen from the body, and each joint’s angle, speed and
torque) and the 12 joint commands. These data are 0.13% of
what the original model was trained on.
Simulator and rebuild.The network is RWM’s: two layers
of gated recurrent units (GRU, a standard recurrent layer) of
width 256 read the last 32 states and joint commands and
predict the change to the next state. RWM trains it on its own
8-step rollouts (rollout training); its released checkpoint has
five output heads on one shared trunk, whose spread is the
uncertainty RWM-U uses. Our rebuild reproduces the release’s
outputs bit for bit and its gradients exactly, and avoids two
defects of the release: training windows spliced across episodes,
and evaluation commands fed one step late (appendix A1).
Held-out data and pools.Each held-out episode yields two
non-overlapping trajectories of 32 history and 368 forecast steps
(7.36 s). Themain pool(F18) holds out episodes 1 and 8 (four
trajectories) and has 97 simulators. Thefive-split pool(X5)
holds each episode out once (20 trajectories, 255 simulators,
each scored on its own split).
Models.The main pool has 48 rollout-trained models: five
seeds of the released configuration (history M= 32 , training
horizon N= 8 ) and variants such as ensembles, a likelihood
loss and other (M, N)settings. 9 were trained one step ahead,
byteacher forcing[ 31] (each step predicted from the true
previous state, never from the model’s own prediction) or
with N= 1 . The other 40 are compressed copies, made after
training: weights rounded to 16 or 8 bits, the smallest 10–
50% of weights set to zero (pruning), or GRU units removed.
The five-split pool repeats the main training methods and
compressions on every split. The main pool also holds teacher-
forced and rollout-trained models trained four times longer, and
study AR used 38 simulators of three other architectures trained
on F18’s split. Training used the release’s loss and Adam for
2,500 iterations unless stated, on a CPU (appendix A1).

state numbers, each divided by its standard deviation in the
data. Eh is the mean error over h-step rollouts started, after
32 true history steps, from every fourth held-out position ( E1:
every position);E 368 uses one 368-step rollout per trajectory.

change. Two checks arelabel-free: they need the history and the
joint commands but no recorded future state. Thegrowth rate

Statistics and registration.Intervals are 95% percentile
intervals from 20,000 bootstrap draws over the independent
unit: whole trajectories for comparisons of methods [ 33, 34],
models for correlations. A result isregisteredif its decision
rule was committed to version control before the measurements
that test it were run [ 35]; a rule gives a verdict only when
its interval clears a stated threshold (usually zero), and “not
settled” otherwise. Three registrations were made for this paper:
the comparison of checks on both pools (primary Q1–Q2,
secondary Q3–Q6), study FT on fine-tuning pruned copies
(FT1–FT2), and studies CL and AR on policy commands and
other architectures (CL1–CL2, AR1–AR2). Primary questions
are judged at 97.5%. Two parts of the first were known from
earlier analyses (appendix A2). Other verdicts come from rules
registered for earlier, unpublished analyses; those registrations
and all code are in the anonymised supplementary material.
Secondary verdicts are judged at 95% without correction;
Holm’s correction over the 7 secondary tests of Q3–Q6 is
exploratory. Everything else is descriptive, or exploratory where
it was added after the registered results were seen. Resampling
parents and trajectories together widens the intervals by a
median factor of 1.5 (exploratory, appendix A4).

Stability.The growth rate tracks the training horizon: it is
positive for N= 1 (+0.61/s) and falls through +0.44, −0.43
and −1.13 to −1.38/s at N= 32 (descriptive; a rule committed
before that analysis found the same order, appendix A1). Every
rebuilt model trained on its own rollouts of eight or more steps
was contracting, and no teacher-forced one was. The released
model does not fit: trained on about 750 times more data, it has
λ1 = +0.70/s on these trajectories, which it may have trained
on (appendix A6). The account therefore covers only models
trained on this data set.

V. DISCUSSION
Why one-step checks mislead here.A one-step check feeds
a simulator true recorded states, inputs it never produces itself.
In use it reads its own predictions, so what matters is how
it treats its own errors. Teacher forcing and one-step training
never show the network those errors, and the simulators they
produced amplify them ( λ1 >0 ). Pruning without retraining
may add a small error at each step that accumulates over
the rollout. One-step error ranked training choices backwards,
while it ranked copies of the same parent well (exploratory).
A checklist from this case study.(1)Train and validate at
the horizon of use, on held-out episodes, counting independent
trajectories. (2)Fine-tune after pruning, and check the pruned
copy at the horizon of use. (3)Without recordings, screen
with label-free checks and know their limits: on the main pool
the residual ranked RWM-style simulators best with recorded
commands, though its lead over 8-step error failed multiple-
test correction and AR2 was not settled, and less well under
a policy; a positive growth rate flagged the one-step-trained
simulators, a negative one certifies nothing, and growth rates
did not rank different architectures together. (4)Read ensemble
spread as a ranking; rescale it per horizon on held-out episodes
before using it as an interval, and do not rely on label-free
run-time alarms: in earlier registered analyses a growth-rate
signal lost to a step counter, and re-synchronising on a score
added error against a fixed schedule (appendix A6).
Limitations.All evidence comes from one robot, one trotting
gait on flat ground and ten 20-second episodes of simulated
data, so “held out” means other velocity commands of the
same gait. The policy drove the simulators only in imagination:
no policy was trained, and nothing was tested in a physics
simulator or on hardware, so what a mis-ranked simulator costs
a controller is not measured, and imagined return is not walking
performance. Under policy commands the checks are compared
with error under recorded commands. The main pool has four
held-out trajectories, which bounds every interval. Correlations
treat models as independent although they share parents, data
and test trajectories; wider intervals that allow for this leave
3 of 13 registered verdicts of the two GRU pools unresolved
(exploratory). The growth rate is a finite-time exponent in the
network’s own coordinates, and the stability account does not
cover the released model. Each other architecture has 12 or
13 runs (3 teacher-forced MLP runs diverged under recorded
commands), and the longer-training check has 3 seeds and no
intervals.
VI. CONCLUSION
On this simulator, one-step checks favoured teacher forcing,
one-step training and a pruned model that, without fine-tuning,
loses most of its skill by 2 s. Without recorded futures, on the
main pool the growth rate ranked RWM-style simulators better
than one-step error but was not separated from 8-step error,
and a negative value certifies nothing; its replication on the
five-split pool failed. The kinematic residual ranked them best
with recorded commands (descriptive; its lead over 8-step error
failed multiple-test correction) and less well under a policy.
Whether these checks predict how well a policy trained inside
the simulator walks is the next question.
