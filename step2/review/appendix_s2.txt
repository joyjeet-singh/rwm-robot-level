A hand-built MuJoCo ANYmal D
Scope.The physics test (Sec. 4.2, appendix A12) uses a model of the robot that we wrote for it. It is not
the simulator that produced the recorded data, and it is not hardware: every statement about walking in
this appendix and in appendix A12 is about this model. All of this appendix is validation of the model,
fixed or measured before the physics test was registered; the held-out errors are descriptive.
Build.A script converts the manufacturer’s published ANYmal D robot description (a URDF file
under a BSD licence) into a MuJoCo model: a free-floating trunk and 12 hinge joints, 57.03 kg in all,
with inertias summed from the description in double precision. MuJoCo’s own URDF import moves the
robot’s centre of mass when it merges fixed parts, so the parts are kept separate. Collision shapes are a
box trunk, capsule thighs and shanks and sphere feet, and the robot touches only the ground. The physics
step is 0.005 s and a policy acts every 0.02 s, as in the data. The state that policies and learned simulators
read (body velocities, gravity direction, joint angles, speeds and torques) is computed from the MuJoCo
state by Isaac Lab’s definitions for this task.
Motor model.The planned motor model, the ANYdrive actuator network that Isaac Lab uses for
this robot, could not be obtained, so the fallback fixed in advance was used: a PD controller with a
fixed delay, which turns each joint command into a torque. It was fitted by least squares to the recorded
joint torques of the 8 training episodes, on steps whose torque was not saturated: Kp = 82.6 N m/rad,
Kd = 4.93 N m s/rad and a delay of 20 ms (4 physics steps), with R2 = 0.93 (root-mean-square error
3.4 N m). The torques separate the delay only coarsely; the shortest delay of the best-fitting group was
kept. Torque is clipped by Isaac Lab’s torque–speed limit for this motor (120 N m saturation, 80 N m
limit, 7.5 rad/s).
Calibration.Four constants were chosen on a fixed grid of 108 points: foot friction (0.6 to 1.2),
the contact time constant (0.005 to 0.02 s), joint armature (0 to 0.05 kg m 2) and joint damping (0 to
1 N m s/rad). Each point replayed the recorded joint commands from recorded states on the training
episodes only, and the point with the lowest mean 8-step error was kept, ties broken by one-step error.
It was frozen before any held-out number or policy was measured. The chosen point has friction 0.6,
time constant 0.01 s, armature 0.02 kg m2 and damping 0.0, with training-episode errors E8 = 0.17 and
E1 = 0.10 (worst grid point: E8 = 0.23). MuJoCo raises a time constant below twice the physics step to
that floor, so the grid’s 0.005 s points behaved as 0.01 s; the exact tie this caused was broken towards
the larger value, by a rule fixed before the grid was run. The uncalibrated model has friction 0.8, time
constant 0.02 s and no armature or damping.
Error on held-out trajectories (descriptive).Table 16 gives the model’s own error when it replays
the recorded joint commands from recorded states on the 4 held-out trajectories of episodes 1 and 8, with
the measures and windows of the main study. No window became unstable. The calibrated model is
better than the floor up to 24 steps but worse at 100 steps: run open loop on recorded joint commands, its
trajectories drift away from the recording, as the learned simulators’ do. It is a separate physics model in
which a policy can walk, not a copy of the data’s simulator.

A12 Policies trained in the simulators
Scope.Every result here is about the hand-built MuJoCo model of appendix A11, not the data’s simulator
and not hardware, and about 9 versions of one learned simulator.
Registration.The design, the two gates and the calibration grid were fixed in a plan committed
before any MuJoCo number existed. The test’s rules and analysis code were committed at 2026-11-07
23:03 UTC (commit b51a8e5), after Gate 1 and the pilot and before any registered policy was trained.
Measurement.A policy is run in the model on the velocity-command schedules of the ten recorded
episodes (20 s each; each recorded segment’s command held at its mean, as in appendix A8), from eight
starts per schedule (joint angles moved by up to ±0.05 rad, random heading), 80 episodes in all. An
episode ends at a fall, by Isaac Lab’s rule for this task (a contact force above 1 N on the trunk). The
primary outcome,physics return, is the lite training code’s own reward computed from MuJoCo states
and foot contacts and averaged over the episodes: rewards for tracking the commanded speed and turning
rate and for feet in the air, and penalties on vertical and tilting body motion, torque, joint acceleration,
changes of joint command, thigh contacts and moving when told to stand. The secondary outcomes are
the fall rate (share of episodes ending in a fall) and the speed and turn tracking errors. A simulator’s score
is the mean over its two policies.
Pilot (Gate 2; validation, not results).Four policies, one seed each, were trained by the authors’ lite
code and measured: one in the released model, and three in rebuilt single-network simulators of the main
pool (the released configuration’s seed 0, a teacher-forced model, and the 50%-pruned copy of seed 0).
The gate asked that the released-model policy fall in fewer than 25% of episodes (P1), that at least one
rebuilt-simulator policy fall in fewer than 50% (P2), and that the four physics returns not all lie inside
each other’s intervals (P3). P1 and P3 passed. P2 failed: all three rebuilt-simulator policies fell in every
episode (Table 18), although their imagined returns were higher than the released-model policy’s. Why
was not studied; one difference is that a single-network simulator gives the lite code no spread between
heads, so its uncertainty penalty is inactive. By the rule fixed in advance, the registered pool became the
fallback pool instead of 25 main-pool simulators.

Pool and training.The pool has 9 simulators: the released five-head checkpoint and eight copies
made from it without retraining (every parameter rounded to 16 bits; weights rounded to 8 bits; 10, 20, 30
and 50% of weights pruned; GRU width cut to 192 and 128 units). All have five heads, so the uncertainty
penalty is active alike. For each, two policies (seeds 1 and 2; 18 in all) were trained by the authors’ lite
code, unchanged apart from small compatibility changes, for 500 iterations with 4,096 imagined robots,
starting from states of all ten recorded episodes, on which the released model was trained. Each policy
took 1.8 to 2.7 hours on a CPU, and the whole job 21 hours. Every policy finished training with finite
losses and was measured; none failed. The checks were computed before registration, with the main
study’s code, on all 20 recorded trajectories: they are in-sample, because the released model was trained
on every episode.
