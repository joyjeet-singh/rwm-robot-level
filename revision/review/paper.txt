One Step Ahead Is Not Enough: Testing a Learned Quadruped
Simulator at the Horizon It Is Used
Anonymous Author(s)
Abstract
Learned simulators let legged robots learn by running a network on its own predictions for tens
to hundreds of steps, yet are often trained, compressed and checked one step ahead. We rebuilt the
open quadruped simulator of the Robotic World Model (RWM), matched its gradients exactly and
measured 101 trained simulators, 200 compressed copies and the released model. One-step checks
reverse the training choices: teacher forcing and one-step training are better one step ahead but 2.0
and 2.6 times as wrong at 2 s, consistent with RWM’s ablation. Pruning 30% of weights keeps 90%
of the skill one step ahead, 39% at 2 s; 500 fine-tuning iterations restored it (registered). The released
ensemble’s spread is too small, increasingly with horizon: the implemented loss is lowest at zero
spread. In a registered comparison over 97 simulators, a kinematic-consistency residual ranked the
2-second error best; on a second pool it was level with an 8-step check (exploratory). The growth
rate beat one-step error but failed its registered replication on mostly compressed copies, probably
because moderate pruning adds error but barely changes it. Scope: simulated data of one robot and
gait, recorded commands, no policy trained. We distil a vetting checklist.
Keywords:learned simulators, world models, legged robots, model-based reinforcement learning,
Lyapunov exponents, model compression, uncertainty
1 Introduction
A legged robot can learn to walk inside alearned simulator: a neural network that predicts the robot’s
next state from its recent states and motor commands, run forward on its own predictions, as image-based
world models are [ 1]. Such a run is arollout, and its length is itshorizon; because each prediction
becomes the next input, small errors can grow [ 2–5]. The Robotic World Model (RWM) [ 6] learns
such a simulator for an ANYmal D quadruped and imagines 24 steps per policy update; RWM-U [ 7]
trains a walking policy inside 100-step imagined rollouts. RWM trains on its own 8-step rollouts and
reports multi-step error. Its ablation found that this autoregressive training significantly outperforms its
teacher-forcing counterpart, and that the forecast horizon plays a decisive role in long-term accuracy [6,
Sec. 4.3, App. A.4.1].
Elsewhere, one-step checks remain common. Offline model-based methods build on ensembles
of one-step models [ 8, 9], and RAMBO keeps the members with the lowest error on held-out single
transitions [10]. Compression [ 11] and uncertainty estimates [ 8, 12] are judged one step at a time,
although one-step accuracy need not track what a model is used for [13]. We askwhen a one-step check
gives the wrong answer about a simulator used 100 steps ahead, andwhether a cheap check without
recorded future states does better.
We answer on RWM’s open-source dynamics model, in a narrow setting: one robot, one trotting
gait, simulated data, recorded commands replayed and no policy trained. We rebuilt the model, matched
the release’s losses and gradients exactly, and measured 101 trained simulators, 200 compressed copies
and the released model. The decision rules of the main comparison were committed before this study’s

2 Related Work
Compounding error.Training on a model’s own predictions is an established remedy for errors that
grow along a rollout [2, 3, 14, 15], as is limiting rollout length [5]; one-step likelihood need not track
control performance (objective mismatch[ 13]). We ask which check predicts the growing error, and
why.Uncertainty for model-based control.Ensembles [ 12, 16] estimate uncertainty; offline methods
keep the members with the lowest held-out one-step error [ 10], penalise imagined reward by their
disagreement [7, 8], and compare penalties by how well they track error [ 17]. Recalibration corrects
predicted uncertainty on held-out data [18, 19]; our per-horizon multiplier is one such correction.Stability
of recurrent networks.Products of a recurrent network’s Jacobians govern exploding gradients [20];
contracting recurrent models are easier to train [ 21]; Lyapunov spectra characterise chaotic recurrent
networks [22] and their training difficulty, which modified teacher forcing addresses [23, 24]; and stable
dynamics can be learned by construction [25]. We use the leading exponent as a check.Physics and
compression.Kinematic consistency has diagnosed long-horizon failure of imagined rollouts [26], like
parity relations [27]; compressed world models are judged by task success [28] or error growth [29], and
pruning is often matched by small networks trained from scratch [30].
3 System and Protocol
Robot data.The release contains 10,000 recorded steps of an ANYmal D trotting on flat ground at 50 Hz:
ten 20-second episodes in which the commanded velocity appears to change once. Every episode starts
from an exact reset, so the data evidently come from a physics simulation rather than the hardware. Each
step holds a 45-number state (the body’s linear and angular velocity, the direction of gravity seen from
the body, and the angle, speed and torque of each of the 12 joints) and the 12 joint commands. These data
are 0.13% of what the original model was trained on; the released model saw about 750 times as much.
Simulator.The network is RWM’s: two layers of gated recurrent units (GRU, a standard recurrent
layer) of width 256 read the last 32 states and commands (thehistory) and predict the change to the next
state; side heads predict foot contacts and do not feed the forecast. RWM trains it on its own 8-step
rollouts (rollout training); its released checkpoint has five output heads on one shared GRU trunk, whose
spread is the uncertainty RWM-U uses.
Rebuild and verification.Before any training, our rebuild’s outputs equal the release’s bit for bit,
and its losses and gradients differ by 0 across the 7 loss terms and 106 parameter tensors of the released

objective. The release has two defects. Its termination column is always zero, so 352 of the 9,961 training
windows splice the end of one episode onto the start of the next; we recover the boundaries from the reset
rows. And its evaluation feeds each command one step late. This raises the released model’s one-step
error most, by 55% [26%, 93%] over causal scoring. At 100 steps the change, 8.2% [−10%, 25%], is not
resolved on the 20 trajectories of Sec. 4, but it is on the four held-out trajectories of episodes 1 and 8
(22% [6.3%, 42%], in the release’s relative error).
Models.On F18 we use 48 rollout-trained models: five seeds of the released configuration, with
history M=32 and training horizon N=8 ; three five-head ensembles; five trained with a likelihood
objective; five independent width-124 models with, together, as many parameters as one five-head
ensemble (a capacity-matched control); three each on the release’s spliced windows and on as many
duplicated windows; three trained four times longer; and three seeds of each of seven other (M,N)
settings. 9 were trained one step ahead: threeteacher-forced[ 31] (every training step predicted from the
true previous state, never from the model’s own prediction), three teacher-forced four times longer, and
three with N=1 (rollout training whose rollouts last a single step). The last 40 are compressed copies
of the five released-configuration models. Compression is applied after training, without fine-tuning:
weights rounded to 16-bit floats or to an 8-bit grid, the 10–50% smallest weights set to zero, or GRU
units removed to width 192 or 128. On X5 each split has five rollout-trained models, three rollout-trained
ensembles, three teacher-forced models and the eight compressed copies of each rollout-trained model
(51 per split). Training used Adam with learning rate 1×10 −4, weight decay 1×10 −5, batches of 256,
gradient clipping none and the release’s 7 loss terms, for 2,500 iterations unless stated, on a CPU without
a graphics card (appendix A1).
Held-out arenas.Overlapping windows are not independent evidence, so each held-out episode
yields two non-overlapping trajectories of 32 history and 368 forecast steps (7.36 s). Most models were
trained with episodes 1 and 8 held out (F18: 4 independent trajectories); the main training methods were
also trained on five fixed splits that hold each episode out once (X5: 20 trajectories, each model scored on
its own split’s four).
Measures.Error is the mean absolute error over the 45 state numbers, each divided by its standard
deviation in the data; every error here is in this measure unless named otherwise. Eh is the error over
h-step rollouts started from every fourth position of the held-out trajectories after 32 true history steps
(E1: every position); E368 uses the single 368-step rollout per trajectory. Thefloorpredicts no change,
holding the last history state. A compressed model’sskill retainedis K(h) =1−∆(h)/S(h) , where ∆
is its extra error over its uncompressed parent and S the parent’s advantage over the floor; K<0 is
worse than predicting no change. Thegrowth rate λ1, the leading finite-time Lyapunov exponent, is the
average exponential rate per second at which the gap between two copies of the simulator, started from
almost identical internal states and driven by the same commands, grows ( λ1 >0 ) or shrinks ( <0 , a
contractingsimulator). We compute it over each 368-step rollout by Benettin’s method [ 32], from exact
Jacobian–vector products in double precision and fixed start directions. Thekinematic residual ¯rJ asks
whether predicted joint angles change as the predicted joint speeds say (trapezoid rule), each joint in units
of the recording’s own residual, averaged over the rollout. Both arelabel-free: they need the history and
the recorded commands but no recorded future state. We also report joint-angle error in degrees and the
error in planar body position, dead-reckoned identically from predicted and recorded body velocity and
yaw rate.

models for correlations. A result isregisteredif its decision rule was committed to version control before
the measurements that test it were run [35]; a rule reads a verdict only when the interval excludes zero,
and “not settled” otherwise. The comparison of Sec. 4.2 was registered for this paper: committed before

4 Results
4.1 Where one step and the horizon of use disagree
Table 1 compares one step with the horizons of use, 24 and 100 steps (0.48 and 2 s), and 368 steps (7.4 s).
Training choices.The first two rows of Table 1 independently confirm RWM’s ablation [6] on 0.13%
of its data: rollout training beats teacher forcing at the horizon of use, and a longer training horizon helps.
What they add is the one-step view, where the order reverses. Teacher forcing wins one step ahead, as
an earlier registered analysis also found. Here its one-step error is 10% lower than rollout training’s
(descriptive: −0.011 [−0.014, −0.0077], 20 trajectories). By 8 steps the order has reversed on 19 of the
20, and the gap grows with the horizon (Fig. 1); at 368 steps the registered rule found rollout training
better on all 10 held-out episodes (sign test p= 0.0020). Over 2 s the teacher-forced simulator’s joint
angles are off by 7.0◦ against 3.4◦, and it misplaces the robot by 0.84 m against 0.51 m (of 1.5 m travelled).
For position over 2 s, neither simulator beats assuming that the robot keeps its last measured velocity
(0.45 m), a strong baseline here because the commanded velocity changes about once per episode; by
7.4 s rollout training beats it and teacher forcing does not.

one-step error of the nine configurations (0.10) and the lowest 100-step error (0.19). The growth rate

Compression.Setting the smallest 30% of weights to zero without retraining keeps 90% of the
parent’s skill one step ahead but 39% at 100 steps, and by 368 steps it is worse than predicting no change
(Fig. 1). In a registered follow-up (appendix A7), 500 iterations of fine-tuning, with the pruned weights
held at zero, restored the 100-step skill from 38% to 102.2% of the parent’s (15 parents). The fine-tuned
copies were nearly as skilled as their parents fine-tuned the same way (99.9% [99.5%, 100.4%]). Moderate
pruning (10–30%) adds error while barely changing the growth rate. At 30%, λ1 shifts by +0.03/s [−0.02,
+0.07], which the registered rule could not separate from zero; at 10 and 20% the shifts are smaller and
barely resolved (exploratory; appendix A5), and no copy pruned by 10–30% has λ1 >0 . Heavier pruning
and unit removal do raise it (by +0.27/s at 50%, +0.40/s at width 128), and 4 of the 25 copies at width
192 and 3 at width 128 became unstable. Without fine-tuning, removing units fails: width 192 keeps 6%
of the skill one step ahead. In an earlier registered study, copies fine-tuned for 500 iterations kept most
of an equally fine-tuned full-width model’s 368-step skill at width 192 (88% [82%, 92%], three seeds);
at width 128 (47%) the result was not settled. Narrow models trained from scratch were better than the
fine-tuned copies at width 128 and could not be separated from them at width 192. In a registered test of
PyTorch’s 8-bit dynamic quantisation (weights and activations; the release’s relative error), the model
kept 95.2% [91.8%, 100.9%] of its 368-step skill.
Uncertainty.The released five-head ensemble’s spread ranks error well (Spearman +0.90 with the
100-step error, on episodes it may have trained on) but is too small, by a factor that grows with the horizon
(Table 1), so no single constant repairs it. Each head’s own variance collapses: the implemented loss is
squared error on a prediction sampled from that variance, whose expectation,(µ−y) 2 +σ 2, is smallest at
σ=0 . A multiplier per horizon, fitted on nine episodes and scored on the tenth, restores ±1σ coverage

4.2 Which check predicts error at the horizon of use?
Which check ranks simulators by E100, the error over RWM-U’s 100-step horizon? The registered
candidates were the labelled one-step error E1 and 8-step error E8, computed at every held-out position,
and the label-freeλ 1 and ¯rJ.
Across the 97 simulators of F18 (Table 2, Fig. 2), one-step error ranked the 100-step error poorly, the
growth rate well and the 8-step error about as well. The first primary comparison favoured the growth
rate over one-step error; the second could not separate it from the 8-step error. The same held at 368 steps.
At 24 steps, which the 8-step windows nearly cover, the 8-step error ranked ahead of the growth rate.
Without the one-step-trained models (88 simulators), the growth rate still ranked better than one-step
error (+0.27 [+0.04, +0.51]), though not under the wider intervals. The kinematic residual ranked best
of all; the registered rule favoured it over the 8-step error (+0.12 [+0.02, +0.23]); this margin does not
survive Holm’s correction (exploratory).
Three exploratory analyses qualify this (appendix A3). With both label-free checks limited to the
target’s 100 steps, the residual still ranks well (+0.94 on F18, +0.96 on X5; exploratory). Over 8 steps it
is level with the 8-step error on F18 (−0.006 [−0.076, +0.063]) and below it on X5 (−0.073 [−0.120,
−0.040]; exploratory). The growth rate needs the long rollout: over 8 steps it ranks at +0.21 on F18 and
−0.02 on X5 (exploratory). Checks and target share trajectories, and every 100-step window begins with
an 8-step one; computed on different halves of the trajectories, each check’s correlation falls by at most
0.04 from its same-half value (exploratory).
Split by what differs between simulators (exploratory), one-step error ranks the 57 trained models of
F18 backwards ([−0.71,−0.20]), while every check ranks the compressed copies well.
The registered replication on the five splits failed for the growth rate. There, where 40 of every
51 simulators are compressed copies, one-step error ranked better than the growth rate (−0.12 [−0.22,
−0.03]), and the 8-step error better still. Under the wider intervals the first of these readings is no longer
resolved, but the growth rate still did not beat one-step error; nor did it without the split that repeats
F18’s held-out pair (−0.14 [−0.26, −0.04]; exploratory, appendix A4). The likely cause is that moderate
pruning adds error while barely changing the growth rate, so a negative growth rate certifies nothing:
of the 17 F18 simulators worse than holding the last state at 100 steps, 15 had λ1 ≤0 . The released
model shows the converse. Rollout-trained on about 750 times more data, it has a low one-step error
(0.083; floor 0.21) but is not contracting on these trajectories (+0.70/s), which it may have trained on. The
stability account of Sec. 4.1 is therefore shown only for models trained on this data set. The kinematic
residual, registered on F18 only, also ranked the five-split pool best, level with the 8-step error (+0.01
[−0.01, +0.03]; without the split {1, 8}, level or slightly ahead: +0.02 [+0.00, +0.05]; exploratory). Every
check here was tested with recorded commands replayed; whether the label-free checks rank simulators
when a policy supplies the commands is untested.
5 Discussion
A checklist for vetting a learned robot simulator.(1)Train for the horizon of use.Rollout training
over eight or more steps gave rebuilt simulators that damp their own errors; one-step training gave
ones that amplify them. (2)Validate at the horizon of use, on held-out episodes, counting independent
trajectories: one-step scores mis-ranked the training objective and passed a pruned model that has lost
most of its skill by 2 s (fine-tuning repaired it). (3)Without recordings, check consistency and stability.

Limitations.All evidence comes from one robot, one trotting gait on flat ground and ten 20-
second episodes of simulated data, so “held out” means other velocity commands of the same gait.
Recorded commands are replayed with no controller in the loop and no policy trained, so what a mis-
ranked or miscalibrated simulator costs a controller is not measured. F18 has four independent held-out
trajectories and X5 twenty, which bounds every interval. Correlations across models treat models as
independent although they share parents, training data and test trajectories; wider intervals that allow
for this (exploratory) unresolve 3 registered verdicts. The growth rate is a finite-time exponent in the
network’s own coordinates and depends on the start direction by tenths of 1/s, and the stability account
does not cover the released model. Only the 30%- and 50%-pruned copies were fine-tuned in this study
(appendix A7), and the 8-bit variant rounds weights only. The registered replication of the growth-rate
comparison failed on the five-split pool, and the kinematic residual’s standing there is exploratory.
6 Conclusion
On this simulator, one-step checks favoured teacher forcing, one-step training and a pruned model that,
without fine-tuning, loses most skill by 2 s, and understated the ensemble’s overconfidence. Without
recordings, on this robot, a kinematic residual ranked simulators best (level with the 8-step error on the
five-split pool; exploratory), and a positive growth rate flagged the teacher-forced and one-step-trained
simulators. Neither replaces a recording at run time or has been tested in closed loop with a policy.

A7 Study FT: fine-tuning the pruned copies
Design.Study FT asked whether a short fine-tuning, with the pruned weights held at zero, restores
the long-horizon skill that magnitude pruning removes; its rules and analysis code were committed
(2026-11-01 22:05, local time) before any fine-tuned pruned model existed. The parents were the 15
rollout-trained five-split models with seeds 0–2, the seeds for which an earlier study had fine-tuned
unpruned controls. Each parent’s smallest 30% of weight-matrix entries (423,283) were set to zero, and
the copy was fine-tuned for 500 iterations by the controls’ protocol (the parent’s training episodes, rollout
loss, settings and seed, fresh optimiser state), with the pruned entries reset to zero after every step; 50%
pruning (705,472 entries) was a secondary arm. The parent, the copies before and after fine-tuning and
the control were all scored on this machine by the measures of Sec. 3, on each parent’s own split, giving
20 trajectories with seeds averaged within each. Two primary questions at 100 steps, with 97.5% intervals
from 20,000 trajectory resamples, were registered: FT1, the change in K against the original parent from
fine-tuning (interval above 0: fine-tuning restores skill); and FT2, K of the fine-tuned copy against the
control, with the earlier study’s thresholds (lower end above 90%: nearly as skilled; above 50%: keeps
most; upper end below 50%: loses most).
